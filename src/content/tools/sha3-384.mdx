  - q: >-
        Why is SHA3-384 noticeably slower than SHA3-256?
    a: >-
        Its capacity is 768 bits, leaving a rate of 832 bits — only 104 bytes absorbed per permutation call, against 136 for SHA3-256. That is about 30% more permutation calls for the same input, and the permutation is where all the time goes.
  - q: >-
        Is SHA3-384 the SHA-3 equivalent of SHA-384?
    a: >-
        Only in output length. SHA-384 is SHA-512 truncated and inherits SHA-512's speed on 64-bit hardware; SHA3-384 is a distinct sponge parameterisation that is slower than its 256-bit sibling. The two produce unrelated digests.
  - q: >-
        When is a 384-bit digest actually required?
    a: >-
        In the 192-bit security tier that pairs with AES-256 and P-384 — the CNSA suite and several government and financial profiles specify it. Outside those, it is over-provisioned for anything a classical attacker can do.

## What SHA3-384 does

SHA3-384 produces a 384-bit digest, written as 96 hexadecimal characters. It runs the
Keccak-f[1600] permutation over a sponge with 768 bits of capacity and 832 bits of rate,
absorbing 104 bytes of input per permutation call.

## The cost of a bigger digest

In SHA-2, moving from SHA-256 to SHA-384 can make things *faster*, because SHA-384 is built
on the 64-bit SHA-512 core. In SHA-3 the relationship is monotonic and intuitive: bigger
digest, bigger capacity, smaller rate, more permutation calls, slower. SHA3-384 costs roughly
30% more per byte than SHA3-256.

That makes the choice straightforward. Pick SHA3-384 when a specification demands a 192-bit
security tier, not as a general upgrade.

## Post-quantum framing

The usual argument for 384 and 512-bit digests is Grover's algorithm, which halves the
effective preimage strength of a hash. A 384-bit digest keeps a 192-bit preimage margin under
that model. Whether this justifies the throughput cost depends entirely on how long the data
must stay secret — for anything with a horizon under a decade, SHA3-256 is not the weak link.
