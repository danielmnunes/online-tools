faq:
  - q: >-
        Should I use DES for anything new?
    a: >-
        No. The key is 56 bits. The Electronic Frontier Foundation's DES Cracker exhausted that space in 56 hours in 1998, on hardware that now fits in a pocket. This page exists because DES ciphertext still turns up, and because test suites still need a DES oracle.
  - q: >-
        Why is the key 8 bytes if DES is 56 bits?
    a: >-
        Each byte stores 7 key bits and 1 parity bit. The parity bits are ignored by the algorithm and by this page. Two keys that differ only in parity encrypt identically.
  - q: >-
        What about triple DES?
    a: >-
        That is a different page. Triple DES applies this cipher three times with two or three keys, which is how DES survived in payment systems after 56 bits became a joke. NIST retired even that in 2023.

## DES, as a historical object

An 8-byte block, a 56-bit key, 16 Feistel rounds. IBM designed it, the NSA
adjusted the S-boxes, NBS published it in 1977, and for two decades it was the
default way a computer encrypted anything.

The S-boxes have no generating formula. They are tables. This implementation
transcribes them from FIPS 46-3 and checks them against the textbook vector
(key `13 34 57 79 9B BC DF F1`, block `01 23 45 67 89 AB CD EF` →
`85 E8 13 54 0F 0A B4 05`) and against OpenSSL, which shares no code with this
page. That is the same standard this site used for bcrypt: if a constant cannot
be derived, it is verified instead.

## Why it is here

Not because 56 bits is enough. Because a page that claims to be a catalogue of
ciphers and quietly omits the one that ran the world for twenty years is a
catalogue of fashion, and because the ciphertext is still out there. Decrypt
it, migrate it, do not produce more of it for any reason other than a test.
