description: >-
    Decrypt AES ciphertext in CBC, GCM, CTR, CFB or ECB. The last 16 bytes of GCM are the tag.
faq:
  - q: >-
        Decrypt failed. What actually went wrong?
    a: >-
        For GCM, almost always the tag: the key, nonce, associated data or ciphertext does not match, and the page refuses rather than returning garbage. For CBC, a padding error usually means the key or the IV is wrong, or the ciphertext was truncated. "Not valid UTF-8" means the bytes came out and they are not text — switch the output to hex.
  - q: >-
        My GCM tag is a separate field.
    a: >-
        Concatenate it onto the end of the ciphertext. That is the shape Web Crypto, noble and this page use. OpenSSL's `enc -aes-256-gcm` writes the tag separately because of a historical accident; the bytes are the same.
  - q: >-
        Why does the IV have to be the same one that encrypted?
    a: >-
        Because the IV is not a second key, it is part of the ciphertext. Losing it is losing the message. CBC needs the original IV to undo the first XOR; GCM and CTR need the original nonce to regenerate the keystream. An IV is not secret. It has to be unique.
  - q: >-
        Can I decrypt if I do not know the mode?
    a: >-
        Not reliably. The same key and IV under two modes produce two different plaintexts, and only one of them is the message. If you have a protocol spec it names the mode. If you do not, CBC-PKCS#7 is the historical default and GCM is the modern one.

## Decrypting AES

The inverse of the encrypt page, with the same modes, the same key sizes and the
same rule that nothing is sent anywhere. What changes is the failure mode:
encryption of well-formed inputs always produces bytes, decryption of a wrong
key produces either a padding error, an authentication failure, or bytes that
are not the message.

GCM is the honest one. A wrong key, a wrong nonce, a wrong associated-data
string or a flipped bit all look the same: the tag does not verify, and nothing
is returned. That is the point of authenticated encryption.

CBC with PKCS#7 is the dishonest one. A wrong key often fails padding, which
sounds useful and is how padding oracles work. Do not treat a padding error as
proof of a wrong key in a protocol; treat it as "this did not decrypt" and
authenticate the ciphertext with something else.

## What to paste

- **Ciphertext** in hex or Base64, which is how it usually arrives.
- **Key** of 16, 24 or 32 bytes.
- **IV / nonce** of 16 bytes for CBC, CTR and CFB, or 12 bytes for GCM.
- **Associated data** for GCM, exactly the bytes that were authenticated, which
  may be empty.

The output is bytes. If they are UTF-8 text they will show as text; if they are
not, the page says so instead of drawing a row of replacement characters.
