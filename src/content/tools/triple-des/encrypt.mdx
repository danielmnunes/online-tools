description: >-
    Encrypt with Triple DES (3DES, TDEA) in CBC or ECB. Two-key or three-key EDE. Retired by NIST in 2023.
faq:
  - q: >-
        Two-key or three-key?
    a: >-
        16 bytes is two-key (K1, K2, K1). 24 bytes is three-key (K1, K2, K3). Two-key is 80-bit work with the sweet-spot attack; three-key is 112. Neither is a reason to start. Payment systems used two-key for years because the key-exchange ceremony was built around 16 bytes.
  - q: >-
        Is this EDE or EEE?
    a: >-
        EDE: encrypt with K1, decrypt with K2, encrypt with K3. That is the construction SP 800-67 specifies, and the one that becomes single DES when all three keys are equal, which is a property EEE does not have.
  - q: >-
        Why did NIST retire it?
    a: >-
        Because 112 bits is below the 128-bit floor everything else moved to, and because a 64-bit block leaks after a few gigabytes of ciphertext under one key (the sweet32 attack). AES has a 128-bit block and no such birthday.

## Triple DES

DES, three times, in encrypt-decrypt-encrypt order. The middle decrypt is not a
mistake: it is what makes a three-key encryption with K1 = K2 = K3 equal to
single DES, so a Triple-DES implementation can talk to a DES implementation by
repeating the key. That backwards-compatibility trick is also why two-key
Triple DES (K3 = K1) exists.

The block is still 8 bytes. The birthday bound on a 64-bit block is what
sweet32 exploited in TLS: after 32 GB or so of ciphertext under one key,
collisions appear and the plaintext leaks. AES does not have this problem. This
page does not make it less true.

Use it to produce a test vector, or to match a payment terminal that has not
been updated. Do not use it to protect anything whose loss you would notice.
