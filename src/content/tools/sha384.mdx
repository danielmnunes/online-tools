faq:
  - q: >-
        Why is SHA-384 often faster than SHA-256 on my machine?
    a: >-
        Because it is SHA-512 underneath, and SHA-512 works on 64-bit words. On any 64-bit processor that means roughly half as many operations per byte as SHA-256, which works on 32-bit words. So despite producing a longer digest, SHA-384 typically hashes large inputs faster than SHA-256 on desktop and server hardware. On 32-bit embedded targets the ranking flips.
  - q: >-
        Is SHA-384 vulnerable to length extension?
    a: >-
        No. SHA-384 is SHA-512 with 128 bits of the final state discarded, and an attacker cannot continue hashing without those bits. This is one of the few places where truncation buys a genuine structural property rather than just a shorter output.
  - q: >-
        Where will I encounter SHA-384 in practice?
    a: >-
        Mostly in TLS cipher suites at the 192-bit security tier, paired with AES-256 and P-384 — the suites required by CNSA and by several government profiles. It also appears in JOSE as the PS384, RS384 and ES384 signature algorithms.

## What SHA-384 does

SHA-384 produces a 384-bit digest, written as 96 hexadecimal characters. It is SHA-512 run
with a different set of initial hash values, with the final two 64-bit words of the eight-word
state dropped from the output.

## The 64-bit advantage

SHA-512 — and therefore SHA-384 — operates on 64-bit words with 80 rounds per 1024-bit block.
SHA-256 operates on 32-bit words with 64 rounds per 512-bit block. Per byte of input, the
64-bit design does substantially less work, so on any processor with native 64-bit arithmetic
SHA-384 usually outruns SHA-256 by a wide margin. That is a counter-intuitive result worth
remembering: the longer digest is often the cheaper one.

The exception is hardware acceleration. Modern x86 and ARM chips ship SHA-256 instructions
(SHA-NI, ARMv8 crypto extensions) but frequently no SHA-512 equivalent, which can reverse
the ranking entirely on those paths.

## Truncation as a feature

Discarding 128 bits of state is what makes SHA-384 immune to length-extension attacks, the
weakness that forces HMAC on plain SHA-256. If you need a hash that can be used more
naively without that footgun, SHA-384, SHA-512/256 and the SHA-3 family are the options.
