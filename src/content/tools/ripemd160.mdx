faq:
  - q: >-
        Why does Bitcoin use RIPEMD-160?
    a: >-
        To shorten addresses. A Bitcoin address is built with HASH160 — SHA-256 followed by RIPEMD-160 — which compresses a public key to 20 bytes. Combining two independently designed hash functions also means a structural break in either one alone does not obviously break the address scheme.
  - q: >-
        Is RIPEMD-160 secure?
    a: >-
        It has no practical collision attack after three decades of analysis, which is a genuinely good record. But 160 bits gives only 80 bits of collision resistance, and 80 bits is within reach of a determined, well-funded attacker in the way that 128 is not. It is fine where it is entrenched; it is not what to pick for new work.
  - q: >-
        How does it differ from the original RIPEMD?
    a: >-
        The original 1992 RIPEMD produced 128 bits and was broken. RIPEMD-160, published in 1996 by Dobbertin, Bosselaers and Preneel, widened the output and restructured the design into two parallel lines of computation whose results are combined at the end.

## What RIPEMD-160 does

RIPEMD-160 produces a 160-bit digest, written as 40 hexadecimal characters. It runs two
independent chains of 80 rounds over each 512-bit block — one using a set of constants and
round functions, the other using different ones — and combines the two results at the end.
That parallel structure is its distinguishing feature, and the reason it has resisted the
attacks that felled MD5, SHA-1 and the original RIPEMD.

## Designed in the open, not by a state

RIPEMD-160 came out of the European RIPE project at KU Leuven rather than from NIST or the
NSA. In the 1990s that provenance was a footnote; after 2013 it became a selling point, and
it is part of why the function retained a constituency long after SHA-1 had displaced it
everywhere else.

## HASH160

In Bitcoin and its descendants the function almost never appears alone. `HASH160` is
`RIPEMD160(SHA256(x))`, applied to a public key to produce the 20-byte payload of a legacy
address or the hash inside a P2WPKH witness program. The layering means an address commits
to the key through two dissimilar functions, and the 20-byte result is short enough for
people to copy around.

## Choosing it today

Only for compatibility. The 80-bit collision bound is the binding constraint, and nothing
about RIPEMD-160 recommends it over SHA-256 or BLAKE3 for a system being designed now.
